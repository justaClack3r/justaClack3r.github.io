Planetary Coupling

A novel two-input, two-output transmission built on the differential behavior of a planetary gearset. The second output's rotation is added on top of the first, so a joint can be driven even while the part it sits on is spinning.

The stacked-input problem

Many mechanisms need to drive something that is already rotating: a tool on a spindle, a joint on a turntable, a cam whose throw you want to change mid-spin. Putting a second motor on the rotating part means slip rings, extra rotating mass and imbalance. If both motors stay stationary instead, the second motion has to pass through the first rotation without getting tangled in it.

How it works

A planetary gearset is a differential. The speeds of its sun, ring and carrier are tied together by the Willis equation:

(ωs − ωc) / (ωr − ωc) = − Nr / Ns ω: angular velocity of the sun (s), ring (r) and carrier (c); N: tooth counts. Fix any two speeds and the third is determined.

The planetary coupling uses that relationship on purpose. It takes two inputs and produces two outputs on concentric shafts:

  • The first output is the first input, unchanged.
  • The second output is the second input added onto the first.

Hold the second input still and the second output turns in lockstep with the first, so the coupling behaves like a solid shaft. Turn the second input and only the relative angle between the outputs changes. A second joint can therefore ride on top of the first, driven from a motor that never moves.

Application: changing eccentricity on the fly

In this prototype, that relative angle sets the eccentricity of a coupling. The offset can be dialed in or out while the power input is spinning, without stopping, unbolting and re-indexing. Dedicated mechanisms exist for this particular job. The planetary coupling is a general solution to the stacked power-input problem, and this is one application of it.

Scaling up

The prototype has two degrees of freedom, but the principle doesn't stop there. Each additional stage adds another concentric shaft and another stacked rotation, so in theory the number of DoF is unbounded. In practice it is limited by tolerances and the strength of the nested concentric shafts: every extra layer thins the walls and adds to the accumulated runout.

It's the same theme as my 3-DoF wrist from the same summer: route rotation through a moving stage from motors that stay put.
