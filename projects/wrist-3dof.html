3-DoF Coaxial-Swerve Wrist

A three-degree-of-freedom wrist with every actuator below the joint. A modified coaxial swerve drive feeds a differential, and a 3×3 kinematic transform I derived decouples the motion for control.

The problem: actuators in the way

A wrist sits at the very end of an arm, the worst place on the robot to put mass. Every motor mounted there is payload the whole arm must carry and inertia every joint behind it must accelerate. The usual fix is remote actuation: keep the motors near the base and transmit motion out to the joint. Doing that for three independent rotations through one compact joint is the hard part.

The mechanism

The design starts from a coaxial swerve module, a mechanism from mobile robotics. Swerve modules steer and drive each wheel independently. In the coaxial variant, the drive shaft runs concentrically through the steering axis, so both motors stay fixed and nothing that rotates has to carry a motor.

I modified it in two ways:

  1. Two output bevel gears instead of one. A standard coaxial swerve has a single bevel pair turning its wheel axle. Mine has two output bevel gears, each driven independently from below.
  2. A differential wrist where the wheel would be. The two bevel outputs drive orthogonal gears, which drive pulleys, which drive a differential wrist. Turning the two inputs together gives one wrist rotation, and turning them in opposition gives the other.

The swerve module's azimuth, its "steering", provides the third degree of freedom. The output shaft gets three independent rotations while all three motors stay below the joint.

Coupling, and the matrices that undo it

The mechanism is coupled. The differential rides on the rotating azimuth stage, so turning the azimuth also turns the differential's gears relative to their drive inputs. Command the azimuth alone and the wrist moves as well, so each motor affects more than one output.

Every connection in the train is a gear or pulley with a fixed ratio, so the coupling is linear and can be written as matrices. I modeled each half of the mechanism as its own matrix, then multiplied the two.

Coaxial section

The coaxial section takes the three motor velocities (ωm1 and ωm2 from the coaxial drives, ωm3 from the azimuth drive) and produces the velocities of the differential's two side gears and of the azimuth. Gc is the gear ratio between each coaxial gear and its side gear, and Ga is the gear ratio of the azimuth.

The Gc/Ga terms in the third column are the coupling: turning the azimuth motor also turns both side gears.

Its determinant, −Gc2/(2Gd), is never zero, so A3dof is always invertible. Inverting it gives the motor velocities needed for any desired wrist motion, so the wrist can be commanded directly in terms of its outputs:

A3dof−1 =1GcGdGc1Ga−1GcGdGc1Ga00−1
For example, a pure azimuth rotation ωaz takes all three motors: the azimuth motor turns at −ωaz while both coaxial motors turn at ωaz/Ga to cancel the coupling.

In the video, each wrist axis is driven on its own with the transform running live.

What to watch for: the output shaft tilting in two directions and the whole head turning in azimuth, all while the three motors stay put in the base. Without the transform, any one of those commands would drag the other two axes along with it.
