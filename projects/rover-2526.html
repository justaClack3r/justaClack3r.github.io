The 2025–26 rover is the Harvard Undergraduate Robotics Club's second competition rover for the University Rover Challenge (URC). I designed its three main mechanical modules: a 6-DoF robotic arm, two grippers and a rocker suspension. They all mount to the welded chassis I designed for the previous year's rover.

The competition

URC, run by the Mars Society, challenges student teams to build a Mars rover prototype and field it at the Mars Desert Research Station in Utah. Missions cover science sampling, retrieving and delivering objects across rough terrain, dexterous servicing tasks on a mock lander, and autonomous navigation. Teams qualify through a written System Acceptance Review and video. A strict mass limit means every subsystem is competing for weight.

Two of those missions drive most of the mechanical design. Servicing needs an arm with real reach, payload and precision. Delivery needs a suspension that can carry payload over rocks and loose ground without tipping or bottoming out.

6-DoF robotic arm

The arm's defining constraint was budget. Instead of compact, purpose-built robot actuators, it had to use affordable, unoptimized motors that are heavy for the torque they make. A heavy motor far out on an arm costs twice: once in payload, and again in the inertia every joint behind it has to swing. So the core design problem became getting motor mass as close to the base as possible.

Two moves to cut distal inertia

  1. Pulley-driven elbow. The elbow motor sits back at the shoulder and drives the elbow through a timing belt that runs the length of the upper arm. Rotational inertia scales with mass × distance², so moving that motor from the elbow to the shoulder removes most of its contribution to what the shoulder must accelerate and hold up.
  2. 2-DoF differential wrist. The final two joints are a differential driven by two motors working together: turning them the same way produces one wrist motion, and turning them in opposition produces the other. Each motor contributes torque to both axes, so the two can be smaller than motors sized to drive one axis alone, and they mount further back on the forearm.

The base uses a stepper motor with a strain-wave (harmonic) reducer. Strain-wave gearing reaches a high reduction in one compact stage with nearly zero backlash. That matters most at the base, where any play is multiplied by the full length of the arm.

Materials and manufacturing

The structure is mostly carbon-fiber links joined by waterjet-cut aluminum plates. Parts that needed tighter tolerances or 3D features were made on a 3-axis CNC mill and a CNC lathe. Brackets, covers and other low-load parts were 3D printed in ABS-CF and Markforged Onyx. The finished arm carries a payload of about 7 kg, more than double the ~3 kg of the 5-DoF arm I designed the year before.

In motion

Lesson carried forward

Backlash in the arm's budget drivetrain was a constant fight. It motivated my Eng-Sci 259 project, BAMF, which identifies backlash and friction from encoder data and compensates for them in the controller. The same priorities, near-zero backlash and seeing both sides of the gearbox, shape the integrated actuator I'm designing now.

Grippers

I designed two grippers this year, each built around a different priority.

G1: Ball-screw linkage gripper

The first design turns the linear travel of a stepper-driven ball screw into parallel jaw motion through a linkage. As the carriage moves along the screw, the linkage swings the two jaws closed while keeping them parallel. The ball screw is efficient and precise, and the linkage geometry sets how jaw speed and force change through the stroke. Most of the gripper is waterjet-cut aluminum.

G2: Rack-and-pinion BLDC gripper

The second design trades mechanical elegance for grip and simplicity. It uses a BLDC motor instead of a stepper, for more torque, and a simple rack-and-pinion drive closes the jaws. The body is mostly 3D printed, so it's quick to iterate and cheap to replace.

Rocker suspension

For 2025–26 I replaced the previous year's six-wheel rocker-bogie with a simpler four-wheel rocker suspension. Each side is a single rigid rocker with a wheel at each end. The two rockers pivot on the chassis and are tied together by a differential bar across the top of the rover: when one rocker pitches up over a rock, the bar pitches the other down by the same amount. All four wheels stay loaded, and the body sits at the average of the two sides' angles, so it pitches roughly half as much over obstacles.

Dropping the bogies removes two wheels, two drive actuators and their pivots, which saves mass and leaves fewer parts to fail. The structure still has to carry the whole rover over rough ground: it withstands more than 90 kg of static load, while the entire suspension, heavy drive actuators included, weighs only about 15 kg.

I checked the rocker assembly with a static structural study in Ansys Mechanical, applying a 500 N load to the bar. Peak von Mises stress was only 14.5 MPa, at the rocker plate where the two legs meet, and most of the structure sits well below that. 6061-T6 aluminum yields at roughly 275 MPa, which leaves a wide margin for impacts and rough terrain.

Integration

All three modules mount to the TIG-welded 6061 chassis I designed for the 2024–25 rover. I sized that chassis for about 80 kg so it could carry the subsystems of both rovers. Because every module was designed inside one top-level assembly, the mounting interfaces and the arm's reach could be checked against the chassis and suspension before any parts were cut.
